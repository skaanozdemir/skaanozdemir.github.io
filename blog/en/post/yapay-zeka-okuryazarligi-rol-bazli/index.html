The EU softened its AI literacy rule, so build training around roles now

After the Digital Omnibus, Article 4 no longer sets a required level, but the duty remains and enforcement began in August 2026. How automation teams can build a small, role-based and recorded training plan.

Analysis·Leadership & teams·October 29, 2026·6 min read·Türkçesini okuyun

The EU has softened the AI Act’s literacy article. According to the European Commission’s Q&A page, updated on 20 October, Article 4 as amended by the Digital Omnibus no longer requires a specific or “sufficient” level of AI literacy. The duty itself has not gone away: providers and deployers of AI systems must still take literacy measures for their staff. And the supervision and enforcement rules have applied since 2 August 2026. My reading is that the legal bar dropped while the business bar stayed where it was. If you treat the change as permission to trim the training budget, you have learned the wrong lesson.

What changed and what didn’t

The Commission’s page, in short:

QuestionAfter the Omnibus
Who is obliged?Providers and deployers of AI systems. The obligation remains.
What level?No specific or “sufficient” level is mandated.
Public sector roleThe Commission and Member States support the effort and publish practical examples.
EnforcementSince 2 August 2026, by national market surveillance authorities rather than the AI Office.
CertificateNot needed. Keeping an internal record of trainings is named as one way to show the work.
High-risk systemsDeployers must still make sure the staff doing human oversight are trained.

Don’t skip the last row. The literacy article got looser, but if you deploy a high-risk system, training the people who oversee it is a separate obligation and it stands. The Commission also sketches a minimum: a general understanding of AI inside the organisation, clarity on whether you act as provider or deployer, and, depending on the risk of the systems in use, what staff need to know about those risks.

A note from someone writing in Türkiye: these rules concern companies that place or use AI systems in the EU market. Check with your legal team whether you are in scope. Even if you aren’t, I think the plan below earns its keep on its own.

The real reason isn’t in the regulation

Gartner’s survey announcement of 19 October is headlined with the finding that only 13% of CIOs say their enterprise has realised significant or transformational value from AI. Gartner’s page refuses automated access, so I am relying on the headline alone. It says enough.

I see most of the value leaking out on the human side. A business unit picks the wrong process. A validation clerk approves model output without questioning it. A manager can’t talk about cost or risk. None of that is fixed by a licence. As I have said publicly, in automation that is increasingly powered by AI the job is not only building bots; it is choosing the right use cases, designing architectures that scale and delivering work with real business impact. Each of those three has a training counterpart.

A role-based plan: who needs to know what

Over the years I have designed UiPath and Blue Prism curricula, from beginner to advanced, for clients and for internal teams, and built teams of developers, business analysts and trainers from the ground up. The most common mistake I see is giving everyone the same course. One slide deck for the whole company is easy to run and easy to count, and rarely changes how anyone works. The Commission’s own wording, which asks for measures that reflect differences in staff knowledge and the context of use, points the same way.

I would build the plan around five roles:

  1. Executive and process owner. Which process suits AI and which is cheaper with a rule-based robot? Which number will prove the value? How does model cost grow with volume? A short workshop built on two real cases.
  2. Business user and citizen developer. What can they automate themselves and what should go to the central team? Which data must never reach a model? A hands-on programme spread over several weeks.
  3. Developer. Writing prompts and evaluation sets, validating model output, logging and error handling. Ongoing, tied to code review.
  4. Human-in-the-loop operator. Where the model tends to get fields wrong on the validation screen, and what to check before pressing “approve”. Short sessions, repeated, with real documents.
  5. CoE and governance team. Which system sits in which risk class, who keeps the records, who gets called when something goes wrong.

The fourth role is the one most often forgotten, and it is also the role the Act still singles out for high-risk systems. In document AI work, human validation exists to catch the places where the model can be wrong. If the operator doesn’t know what to look for, the control exists only on paper.

Citizen developers: fast value, fragile structure

In February 2026 BOTTEQ started a 10-week UiPath Citizen RPA Developer programme at a large holding company, with the aim of growing the number of citizen developers inside it. I have written before about why I like the approach: combined with the right use-case selection and a sustainable architecture, it creates fast, measurable value in business units.

The condition sits in the second half of that sentence. I think citizen developer training has to teach two things: how to use the tool and where its limits are. Once AI components enter the picture, the second matters more. A business user knowing why sending customer data to an external model is a problem is more important than knowing how to drag an activity onto the canvas.

Records: no certificate, but a trail

The Commission says plainly that no certificate is needed and mentions keeping an internal record of trainings. My suggestion is to attach that record to something already alive. Your automation inventory has a row per process. Add to it which role received which training, and when. The record then works as a management tool more than an audit file: you will notice, for instance, when a new validation operator is put on a queue before being trained.

What it costs and when it is too much

Like any recommendation, this one has a price. A role-based plan takes more preparation than a single general course, and it has to be kept current: when the model or the process changes, operator training changes too. For a small team running only a handful of AI components, a five-role programme can be overkill. Two roles usually cover it there: the manager who decides and the person who checks the output.

There is also a case where it is the wrong answer. Training does not rescue a badly chosen use case. If the process is a poor fit for AI, even the best-trained operator ends up correcting a bad system’s mistakes by hand. Pick the case first, then train for it.

One question for Monday

Open your inventory and list the processes that include an AI component. For each, ask: does the person approving this output know which field the model gets wrong most often? If the answer is “we don’t know”, the first line of your training plan is already written.
