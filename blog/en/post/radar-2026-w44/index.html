AI Builder credits end on 1 November, and agents get tested in production

Issue four: the end of AI Builder credits, UiPath runtime evaluations for live agents and a new package feed, Agent Skills moving to the Linux Foundation, GİB changes to e-Müstahsil and UserList, and Gartner’s CIO survey.

Weekly Radar·Governance & operations·October 26, 2026·7 min read·Türkçesini okuyun

This week is about infrastructure that changes quietly. The credit model behind document-processing flows in Power Automate changes on 1 November, UiPath is moving its package feed, and Türkiye’s revenue administration added mandatory fields to the e-Archive report schema. None of it makes headlines. Any of it can mean a robot that doesn’t start one morning.

Microsoft: AI Builder capacity add-ons and seeded credits reach end of life on 1 November

Microsoft Learn · Partner Center announcement 22 October 2026; calendar page 2 October 2026

Microsoft reminded partners that AI Builder capacity add-ons retire on 1 November 2026 and asked them to switch off auto-renew before each customer’s next renewal date. The End of AI Builder credits page gives the wider picture: on the same date, the AI Builder credits seeded into licences such as Power Apps and Power Automate premium also reach end of life. Existing add-ons and seeded credits stay usable until the contract term ends. The AI Builder features themselves aren’t going away; they run on Copilot Credits. An environment consumes AI Builder credits first, then Copilot Credits, and blocks the feature if neither is available. There’s no automatic conversion and no fixed ratio between the two; each scenario has its own rate.

Why it matters. Every Power Automate flow that reads invoices or forms sits inside this change. My reading: the risky date isn’t 1 November but the day your current contract ends. If the environment has no Copilot Credits by then, flows start failing, and the first people to notice will probably be in accounts payable. I’d check two things this week: a list of flows that use AI Builder, and the licence renewal dates. On the budget side you’re moving from a fixed pack to consumption, which is hard to forecast if you don’t know your monthly document volume.

UiPath Agents: runtime evaluations for deployed agents

UiPath Documentation · 20 October 2026

UiPath put runtime evaluations into preview. They score a deployed agent against datasets of inputs and expected outputs, on demand or on a recurring schedule, and they run against the live agent rather than a design-time copy. The aim is to catch regressions, accuracy problems and unexpected behaviour without a manual test cycle. Production runs can be turned into test cases, so the dataset grows with the cases the agent actually sees.

Why it matters. With classic RPA, a robot that worked yesterday works today. Agents don’t give you that guarantee: when the model, the prompt or the data shifts, behaviour can drift. So a recurring exam in production is, in my view, an operating requirement, not a nice-to-have. The silent model swap we covered last week is exactly what this kind of test catches. While it’s in preview I wouldn’t make it a release gate. But start collecting a small golden set from real cases for your critical agents now; it pays off when the feature reaches general availability. It has a cost too: every evaluation run consumes model calls.

Orchestrator: the official package feed is moving to pkgs.uipath.com

UiPath Documentation · 21 October 2026

The official UiPath feed behind Orchestrator’s host Libraries feed is moving from its Azure DevOps address to https://pkgs.uipath.com/official/index.json. The new feed is live and serves the same packages. The reason is Microsoft retiring public Azure DevOps projects and converting the rest to private during 2027, after which the old address requires authentication. Both addresses work in parallel for now; the Azure DevOps one is deprecated and planned for removal by April 2027. Networks that restrict outbound traffic need pkgs.uipath.com on the allowlist, and anyone who hard-coded the old address in tenant settings, machine images or unattended installs should update it.

Why it matters. This is the kind of change nobody puts in a calendar. Banks and insurers usually lock down outbound traffic tightly, and an allowlist change is often a separate security request with its own approval cycle. April 2027 looks far away, but raising the request today is the cheapest option. Search your golden images and install scripts for the old address. If you find it now, the next robot server build won’t surprise you.

Anthropic hands Agent Skills to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation

Anthropic · 22 October 2026

Anthropic is donating Agent Skills, the open standard for giving agents specialised knowledge and workflows, to the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF), which sits under the Linux Foundation. The donation covers the standard, its name and logo, the GitHub organisation and the agentskills.io domain. Anthropic co-founded the AAIF with Block and OpenAI last year and had already donated the Model Context Protocol (MCP) to it. Engineers from Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and other companies will maintain the standard as individuals. The proposed roadmap includes versioned releases, a formal proposal process and security guidance for skill authors and installers. According to the post, more than 70 agent platforms have integrated the standard, and you don’t need to join the foundation to use it.

Why it matters. For enterprises the real question is lock-in. Writing process knowledge once as a skill, say the rules for checking a supplier invoice, and reusing it across agent platforms lowers the cost of switching. MCP did that for tools; Agent Skills promises the same for know-how. One caution: skill files can carry instructions and code. Teams installing third-party skills should review them like any software dependency. Until the security guidance on the roadmap arrives, I think an approved-skills list is a sensible start.

GİB: SMS approval for e-Müstahsil receipts enters the e-Archive report; integrators have until 16 November

Turkish Revenue Administration · 14 and 16 October 2026; UserList guide 30 September 2026

Türkiye’s revenue administration (GİB) updated the e-Archive technical guide to version 1.19 and refreshed the e-Archive package on 14 and 16 October to reflect a change in the e-Müstahsil Makbuzu, the electronic receipt issued when buying produce from a farmer. Instead of the farmer signing a printout by hand, the report now carries the SMS code sent to the farmer’s phone and the phone number. A new mandatory element, operatorUygulamaBilgi, records the name and tax number of the operator that sent the code. I couldn’t find a separate effective date for this change in the guide. Separately, the UserList guide_Kilavuzu_V.1.1.pdf) updated on 30 September publishes taxpayers whose accounts were closed under Communiqué No. 509 in a UserTempList, and the announcement says the related development must be done by 16 November 2026. Under the guide, private integrators close the taxpayer’s e-document accounts from the closure date in the list.

Why it matters. For food and retail companies buying agricultural produce, the producer receipt has been one of the hardest documents to automate because of paper and wet signatures. SMS approval moves that step into digital form, which makes it a good moment to redesign the receipt flow. If you generate the report with your own software, the schema change is your job; if you use an integrator, ask for their release plan. On the UserList side, the practical question I’d ask: how do the robots that decide between e-Invoice and e-Archive learn that a counterparty’s account has closed? It isn’t a deadline for you on its own, but it’s a check that prevents the wrong document type.

Gartner: only 13% of CIOs say their enterprise has realised significant or transformational value from AI

Gartner · 19 October 2026

According to the title of Gartner’s 19 October survey release, only 13% of CIOs say their enterprise has realised significant or transformational value from AI. Gartner’s page blocks automated access, so this item rests on the release title alone; I couldn’t read the sample size or the other findings on the primary page, so I’ve left them out.

Why it matters. The title alone says something: the money has been spent, but in most organisations the value isn’t measurable yet. I think much of the problem sits in use-case selection and measurement. If “value” isn’t defined up front, even a good pilot ends as an impression. This week’s runtime evaluations and credit consumption are two ends of the same problem: quality needs measuring and cost needs to be visible. At the first meeting of your next AI project, ask: which number will we look at in six months to call this a success?
